How can a commercial real estate broker find owners who may sell before they list?

Read the public filings that come before a sale or a distress event: deeds, mortgages, liens, tax status, court filings and pre-foreclosure notices. Join them to each tax lot, grade the owners by how many independent signals point the same way, and show the evidence on every row.

Why do public records work for this?

Owners rarely announce a sale before they call a broker. But most of the events behind one leave a filing in a county office: a loan is recorded, a lien is placed, taxes go unpaid, a lawsuit touches the title. The records are public and dull, and most brokerages never read them because there are too many.

Which records matter?

  • Deeds show who owns a property now, how long they have held it, and whether it moved between related entities.
  • Mortgages show who lent against it and, usually, when the loan comes due. A loan coming due forces a refinance or a sale.
  • Liens show unpaid obligations attached to the property.
  • Tax records show whether property taxes are current.
  • Court filings show disputes that touch the property or its owner.
  • Pre-foreclosure filings show that a lender has started the process of taking it back.

How do I combine them?

One record is a weak signal. A lien alone can be a billing dispute. A loan coming due alone is routine. Three independent records on the same lot are a different picture. Join every record to its tax lot, count the independent signals, and grade each owner by how many agree.

How do I show the evidence?

Every row should say how each signal is known:

  • A record read: the filing itself, with its source.
  • A name classified: for example, an owner type worked out from the name on the deed.
  • A proxy: a stand-in measure used where the real one isn't public.

Keep two labels apart. "No signal" means the lot was checked and nothing was found. "Not checked" means the records could not be read. If they are merged, the list looks cleaner than it is.

How do I check a screen before I trust it?

Test it against cases you already know: owners who sold or went into distress recently. If the screen would not have flagged them, it will not flag the next ones.

Know what that test proves. It shows the screen catches situations that are already visible. It does not prove the list will find sales nobody has spotted yet. Treat the top of the list as calls worth making, and track which ones convert.
