Account targeting and scoring: who to sell to, and the odds you win them

We work with your sales team to define who you should sell to, find what your best deals had in common, and then score every account in your market on those signals, so you know which ones you're most likely to win.

For example

  • Companies whose job posts name the software they are about to replace
  • Buildings whose deed, lien and court history says the owner may sell, graded A to D
  • Acquisition targets ranked by the rates insurers have contracted to pay them

How it works

  1. Define the target. We sit with your sales team and pin down your ideal customer and your real market, using how your deals actually close, not a list of industries and headcounts.
  2. Read your own wins and losses. We go through your closed deals, won and lost, and find what the wins had in common before they bought: a system they were replacing, a loan coming due, a new regulator, a hiring push. Your reps know most of these already. We write them down and test them.
  3. Map the whole market. We build the full list of accounts that could buy, not just the ones already in your CRM.
  4. Score every account. Each one is checked for those signals in public records most firms never read: filings, job postings, enforcement actions, court records. Every signal says how it is known: a record we read, a name we classified, or a proxy. Accounts we couldn't read are marked "not checked", never "no signal".
  5. Rank by the odds you win. You get the list ranked by probability, with the evidence on every row, so a rep can see why an account is at the top before calling.
  6. Keep it learning. As new deals close or fall through, they go back in, and the signals and scores are re-checked against them.

The signal architecture underneath

There isn't a common word for this, so here is what it is. A signal architecture is the system that turns "what our best customers had in common" into a ranked list that stays current. It has five parts.

  1. Signals. Each thing your best customers had in common, written as a rule we can check: "posted a job naming the system they're replacing", "loan due within 18 months". Until your data backs a rule, it stays marked as a hunch.
  2. Sources. For each signal, where it shows up in public: which filing, which job board, which registry. We test that each source can actually be read, and how often it changes.
  3. Evidence. What each source says about each account, kept as found, with the link and the date. What a record says is stored apart from what it means for you, because the same filing can matter to one client and not another.
  4. Scoring. Every account is checked against your rules. The score shows which signals hit, which missed, and which couldn't be checked.
  5. Watching. Sources are re-read on a schedule. When something changes about an account, that account is re-scored and you hear about it. A quiet week is reported as quiet, not padded.

Your wins and losses feed back into the first part, so the rules get sharper the longer it runs.
